Cell-Cycle Modeling

Run pre-modeling QC, identify peaks, choose a model, and interpret fit results.

6. Cell Cycle Modeling

Once a channel is plotted, click Cell Cycle Modeling in the sidebar. The file and channel controls slide away and the sidebar reveals the Pre-modeling QC Filtering box (four filters: Structural, Time, Cell Gate, Singlet Gate — toggle individually or with Run All), the Identify Peaks panel, and the Model & Fit panel; moving these out of the plot panel also gives the histogram more height. QC is a separate action, applied on its own before peak review. Back returns the sidebar to the file/channel controls without losing any progress.

With exactly one sample checked, Identify Peaks reviews that sample; with several checked at once, click a row in the table to choose which one it's reviewing. Detect Peaks runs automatic peak detection and proposes G1 and G2/M regions (when several samples are checked and none is singled out, it detects for all of them); the four numeric fields let you adjust each region's left/right limits directly, with inline validation if an edit breaks their ordering. Reset discards manual edits and restores the automatic proposal; Accept marks the current regions reviewed; Apply to All copies the reviewed sample's regions to every plotted sample.

In Model & Fit, pick a model — the dropdown opens on Model Selection… so the choice is always deliberate. Fit Current fits the reviewed sample; Fit All Samples fits every plotted sample (see below). Fitted G1/S/G2–M fractions are written to the metadata table (one column group per model in use), alongside the accepted G1/G2 peak-region bounds.

Which model should I choose?

PhaseFinder does not pick a model for you. Each one makes different assumptions, and the right choice depends on your data:

ModelWhat it doesChoose it when
Dean–Jett Generative fit: two Gaussian peaks plus a smooth S-phase profile between them, fitted by maximum likelihood against the observed counts. Your sample is asynchronous and the S phase looks like a simple bridge between the peaks.
Dean–Jett–Fox Dean–Jett plus an extra flexible term that lets the S-phase profile take a more complex shape. The S phase is uneven — for example after a release from arrest. This is the most commonly used model.
Watson Pragmatic Not a likelihood fit. Estimates each peak locally from its clean flank, then calls whatever is left over between the two peak centres S phase. You want a fast, assumption-light number, or a sanity check against a generative fit.
Watson Classic The original Watson formulation, retained for comparison with published Watson results. You are reproducing or comparing against an analysis that used classic Watson.
CLOCCS (Unverified) Fits one set of shared timing parameters across a whole strain's timepoints at once, rather than each sample independently. Requires timepoint metadata. You have a synchronised time course and you accept that this model has not yet been validated — treat its output as exploratory.

There is no “Automatic” option

An earlier version offered one that chose between Dean–Jett and Dean–Jett–Fox by an information criterion. It was removed because that comparison cannot be made reliably while the peak positions are held fixed — and reporting a confident automatic choice that rested on an unreliable comparison was worse than asking you to choose.

Watson Pragmatic deserves one extra note: because it takes S phase as leftover signal, that residual is measured strictly between the two fitted peak centres — signal below the G1 centre (sub-G1 debris) or above the G2/M centre (post-G2 aggregates) is never counted as S. Watson models no separate debris/aggregate component, so keep such contaminants outside the G1–G2/M interval. Because it is a decomposition rather than a generative model, its results are deliberately never ranked against Dean–Jett or Dean–Jett–Fox by AIC/BIC.

Comparing models and information criteria (AIC/BIC)

When comparing different models on the same sample (for example, Dean–Jett versus Dean–Jett–Fox), information criteria such as AIC, AICc, and BIC quantify goodness-of-fit while penalizing parameter count to prevent overfitting. However, statistical comparisons carry strict prerequisites:

  • Identical accepted regions and domain: AIC and BIC values are comparable only when models are fitted to the exact same histogram data over the exact same analysis domain and binning. Finalize peak regions in Identify Peaks before comparing models.
  • Avoid per-model peak tuning (AUDIT-009): Iteratively adjusting peak regions for each candidate model until a favored model's fit looks best undermines information criteria, reintroducing a garden-of-forking-paths hazard where candidate models no longer compete on equal footing. Always fix the accepted regions first, then fit alternatives against that shared benchmark.
  • Comparable likelihood formulations: Information criteria are meaningful between likelihood-based generative models (such as Dean–Jett and Dean–Jett–Fox). They cannot be compared against geometric decomposition methods like Watson Pragmatic, nor across different samples or different bin counts.

Single-seed regression vs. calibrated validation (AUDIT-010)

PhaseFinder's internal automated tests evaluate model convergence and parameter recovery against a 62-case deterministic synthetic benchmark with fixed random seeds. While single-seed regression testing verifies that algorithmic implementations do not drift across software updates, a single-seed pass is not a calibrated empirical sampling distribution. Real acquisitions exhibit biological and optical variability that single-seed tests cannot capture; treat published tolerance intervals as regression guards and validate experimental conclusions with biological replicates.

Fitting several samples at once

Fit All Samples does more than loop over the samples. Sharing one set of peak-region boundaries across samples is only valid if those samples share a comparable DNA axis, so PhaseFinder checks before it does so:

  1. It auto-detects peaks for every plotted sample and records the channel, range, and instrument settings each one used.
  2. It excludes samples whose detection failed, was inferred rather than found, or fell below the confidence threshold — and any sample whose DNA axis is not comparable to the others.
  3. From the samples that remain it builds a robust (median) proposal rather than an average, so one atypical sample cannot drag the shared boundaries.
  4. It shows you exactly which samples are included and which will be fitted independently, and waits for you to confirm.
  5. Samples excluded from the shared proposal are still fitted — just using their own detected regions.

Every result records which mode it used, so you can always tell afterwards whether a sample was fitted on shared or independent regions.

Reading a result honestly

A percentage on its own does not tell you whether to trust it. PhaseFinder separates three different questions, and you should check all three before using a number:

  • Did the optimizer converge? A fit that ran out of iterations, stalled against a limit, or failed numerically is reported as not converged. It is not a result.
  • Is the result valid for reporting? Separate from convergence. A fit can converge and still be refused — for example if a parameter is pinned against its bound, too much of the modelled distribution falls outside the histogram, or a required QC step was unavailable.
  • Do the warnings matter for your question? Warnings flag things the fit cannot decide for you: a very complex S-phase shape, a peak sitting near a constraint, unusual residual structure.

Two limits a histogram cannot resolve on its own

When a sample shows only one peak, PhaseFinder assumes it is G1 and places G2/M at twice that position. On a G2-arrested sample that assumption is wrong, and no statistic within a single histogram can detect it — move the regions manually if you know the biology.

Likewise, a G1/G2 pair and a G2/G4 pair have the same 2:1 spacing, so a single histogram cannot tell them apart. Check that the detected G1 sits where you expect it to, and use a stained control or a sample from the same run that clearly shows both peaks.

Evaluating Fit Quality: The Residual Strip

Beneath the main event histogram, PhaseFinder renders an aligned residual strip for each plotted fit on the shared DNA axis:

  • Pearson residuals: Plotted by default ($z = (O - E) / \sqrt{E}$). Because Poisson counting variance scales with expected count $E$, Pearson normalization prevents high-count G1 peaks from visually dominating the residual plot over lower-count S-phase and G2/M regions.
  • Reference band (±2): A shaded reference band highlights the ±2 standard error interval. Bins exceeding ±2 are colored distinctly to highlight local model misspecifications or unmodeled sub-populations.
  • Raw residuals: The controls allow toggling between Pearson residuals and raw counts ($O - E$).

Exporting Model Fits

Click Download in the plot controls to open the export dialog, where you can download your results in several reproducible formats:

  • Machine-readable JSON: Exports full provenance for every plotted sample, including application release, Git commit hash, sample metadata, model identifier and version, applied configuration hash, analysis domain, bin count, accepted peak regions, preflight QC results, fit parameters, phase fractions, goodness-of-fit metrics (RMS error, reduced χ², AIC, BIC), and all optimizer warnings or reporting qualifications.
  • Per-bin CSV: Exports the observed histogram counts, fitted total curve, individual phase components (G1, S, G2/M), and residuals across each channel bin for independent plotting and numerical audits.
  • QC matrix (TSV): Detailed event-level filtering accounting across every QC stage for all loaded files.
  • HTML report & vector images: Comprehensive standalone HTML report or publication-ready SVG and PDF plots.

Pre-modeling QC filters

Automated preprocessing works best in layers rather than a single step: first remove acquisition artifacts over time, then apply cell-cloud (debris) gating and explicit singlet/doublet discrimination, and only then fit the histogram. Order matters because clogs, flow-rate surges, dynamic-range artifacts, and margin events distort the very populations the model is trying to quantify. PhaseFinder's four QC filters implement that order:

  1. Structural QC rejects non-finite, negative, and saturated measurements while preserving original event indexes.
  2. Time QC removes unstable acquisition periods. When you switch it on, you choose a method: Robust summary QC monitors each acquisition bin's event rate, medians, and IQRs; Peak-tracking QC follows the major distribution peaks across overlapping acquisition bins and removes regions where those peaks shift abnormally. The panel then reports the method, events removed, and the acquisition regions it flagged. Use Change… to switch methods and re-run.
  3. Cell Gate fits an FSC/SSC cell-cloud gate. Drag the teal ellipse or its center marker to reposition it, hold Shift or Ctrl while dragging (or Ctrl plus Left/Right arrow when focused) to rotate it around its center, or change Gate coverage to resize it. Each edit immediately updates the mask and invalidates the histogram and any peak regions so they can be rerun with the edited gate. Reset fitted gate restores the fitted center, coverage, and rotation.
  4. Singlet Gate applies DNA pulse-geometry singlet/doublet discrimination when the required channels exist.

The masked histogram is rebuilt automatically whenever QC changes. Pan and zoom remain display-only; to intentionally restrict histogram and model inputs, open the axis-range dialog and select Use the X limits as the scientific analysis domain. A required QC failure or degraded outcome blocks authoritative modeling and remains visible for review.

Works offline

Peak detection and nonlinear fitting are implemented in PhaseFinder's own ES modules, and D3 is vendored locally. The complete pipeline requires no runtime CDN connection.
